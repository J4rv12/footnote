# Set-Piece Playbook — 2026/27 edition

## 1.1 Attacking corners: principles

From the left, the default delivery is an inswinger; from the right, an outswinger. Six players attack the penalty area on every corner. Two players stay on the edge of the box for second balls, and one centre-back stays in our own half with the goalkeeper.

The corner taker waits for the captain's call before running up. A corner taken before the call is a corner wasted, however good the delivery.

## 1.2 Near-post flick routine

The taker drives the ball to the near post at head height. The near-post runner attacks the ball from the edge of the six-yard box and flicks it on towards the far post, where two runners arrive late, one aiming at the six-yard line and one at the penalty spot.

The near-post flick is used against teams that mark zonally on the near post. Its codename is listed in section 6.1.

## 1.3 Short corner routine

The taker plays the ball short to a player who has sprinted from the edge of the box to the corner of the penalty area. The ball goes back to the taker, who crosses from a deeper angle while the defence steps out.

The short corner is used when the opposition has more tall players in the box than we do. Its codename is listed in section 6.1.

## 2.1 Defending corners: setup

We defend corners with a hybrid system. Four players defend zones along the six-yard line, three players mark the opposition's most dangerous attackers man-to-man, one player guards the near post and one player stands on the edge of the box.

The goalkeeper decides whether to come for the ball. If the goalkeeper calls, everyone else gets out of the way.

## 2.2 Defending corners: clearing

The ball is cleared high and wide, never across the face of the goal. Once the ball is cleared, the whole team pushes out to the edge of the penalty area on the head coach's shout, and the near-post player follows last.

Nobody stays on the goal line after the clearance to "keep an eye on things". Offside is our friend.

## 2.3 Defending short corners

When the opposition plays a corner short, the nearest two players to the ball press it immediately: one closes down the receiver, and one blocks the return pass to the taker. The player on the edge of the box steps across to cover the cross.

The rest of the team holds its zones and markers until the ball is crossed. Nobody leaves the six-yard line to chase the short option.

## 3.1 Direct free kicks

Direct free kicks within shooting range are taken by the designated takers: the number 10 for shots from left of centre, the number 7 for shots from right of centre. The head coach may change the takers for a particular match, and announces it at the team talk.

The goalkeeper decides how many players stand in the defensive wall. One player lies down behind the wall for every direct free kick against us within 30 yards.

## 3.2 Crossed free kicks

For a free kick crossed into the box, our back line holds the line set by the deepest attacking player until the taker strikes the ball. Runners start late and attack the space between the penalty spot and the six-yard box.

The taker waits for the captain's call, as for corners.

## 3.3 Free kicks in our own half

Free kicks in our own half are taken quickly, to restart play before the opposition can reorganise. The nearest player takes the free kick unless it is inside our own penalty area, where the goalkeeper takes every free kick.

A quick free kick is not a careless one. If no pass is on, the taker waits for the defence to set.

## 4.1 Throw-ins in our defensive third

In our own third, throw-ins are taken quickly and safely, down the line to the nearest free player. Nobody throws the ball infield across our own penalty area.

If no player is free, the taker waits. A throw-in that is held for five seconds is better than one that becomes a chance for the opposition.

## 4.2 Long throws

In the attacking third, the left-back takes long throws into the box, aiming at the near post. The target player flicks the ball on, as for the near-post flick corner.

The long throw is used only when the head coach signals for it from the touchline. Without the signal, the throw is taken short.

## 4.3 Throw-ins in the middle third

In the middle third, the full-back on that side takes the throw-in. The nearest midfielder shows short, and the winger runs in behind the opposition full-back.

If both options are covered, the ball is thrown back to the centre-back, who switches play to the other side.

## 5.1 Penalty takers

The penalty order for each match is decided by the head coach before kick-off and announced at the team talk. The default order is the number 9, then the number 10, then the number 7.

The player named first takes the penalty if on the pitch. There is no discussion on the pitch, no last-minute volunteering and no taking the ball from a team-mate. The captain enforces this.

## 5.2 Penalty shoot-outs

At the end of extra time, the head coach gives the order of shoot-out takers to the captain. The order is not changed on the pitch.

Players practise penalties at the end of the session on the day before every cup tie. The goalkeeping coach keeps a record of every opposition taker's preferred side.

## 5.3 Penalties against us

Before a penalty against us, the goalkeeper looks to the goalkeeping coach on the bench, who signals the taker's preferred side from the goalkeeping coach's record. The goalkeeper is free to ignore the signal.

Two players stand on the edge of the penalty area, one on each side of the arc, and follow in for any rebound. Nobody encroaches before the ball is kicked.

## 6.1 Corner codenames

Corner routines are called by codename, shouted by the captain and confirmed by the taker raising one arm. The code word for the near-post flick corner drill is VIPER. The short corner routine is MAMBA. The far-post overload, with four runners attacking the back post, is ADDER.

When no codename is called, the taker delivers the default corner from section 1.1.

## 6.2 Free-kick hand signals

Free-kick routines are called by hand signal from the taker, so that the opposition cannot hear them. One hand on the head means a direct shot. Both arms crossed means a dummy run over the ball before the shot. A raised fist means a cross to the far post.

The double-dummy free kick, in which two players run over the ball before the third shoots, is called COBRA and is the only free-kick routine with a spoken codename.

## 6.3 Changing codenames

Codenames and hand signals are changed when the coaching staff believe they have been compromised, for example after a television broadcast with a clear view of the captain's mouth.

New codenames are issued on paper by the analysts and learned by the next training session. Old lists are shredded, not thrown in the bin.

## 7.1 Marking assignments: general

The assistant coach sets marking assignments for each opponent and writes them on the dressing-room whiteboard before the team talk. Each assignment names the opposition player by shirt number and the Inter Mediate player marking them.

The tallest opposition attacker is marked by our tallest centre-back. The opposition's set-piece taker is not marked; the player on the edge of the box watches for the short option.

## 7.2 Communication on set pieces

The goalkeeper commands the six-yard box, and the captain commands everything outside it. Only those two voices are used while the ball is being delivered.

Players do not argue about marking during a set piece. Any disagreement is raised at half-time, quietly, with the assistant coach.

## 8.1 Set-piece training

Set pieces are practised for 30 minutes in the session on the day before every match. Every player in the expected matchday squad takes part, including substitutes, who are told which roles they would take if they came on.

The analysts film every set-piece session from the gantry so that the coaching staff can check positions afterwards.

## 8.2 Video review

The analysts clip every set piece from every match, for and against. The clips are reviewed with the players at the Monday video session.

Players may ask the analysts for their own clips at any time. Clips are not shared outside the club, because they show exactly how our routines work.

## 8.3 Ownership of this playbook

The assistant coach owns this playbook and updates it during the season. Changes are announced at the team meeting before they are used in a match.

Each player receives a printed copy, numbered, at the start of the season, and returns it on leaving the club. A lost copy is reported to the assistant coach the same day.
